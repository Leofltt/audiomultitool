Understanding Guitar Tuning & Pitch Frequencies

Published: August 16, 2026 Category: Instrument Acoustics Reading Time: 7 min

Tuning a musical instrument is one of the most fundamental tasks a musician must perform, yet the physics behind it are highly complex. When you pluck a string, you initiate a sound wave that vibrates at a specific frequency measured in Hertz (cycles per second). An online chromatic tuner uses your device’s microphone to isolate these vibrations and translate them into musical notes.

In this guide, we will explore standard pitch references (such as A440), the mathematical relationships between notes, the exact frequencies of standard and alternate guitar tunings, and how pitch detection algorithms work in real time.

The A440 Standard and Twelve-Tone Equal Temperament

Modern Western music is calibrated to a reference pitch known as **A4 = 440 Hz**. This frequency, established globally in the mid-20th century, defines the pitch of the A note above middle C. All other notes are calculated relative to this standard using a mathematical system called **Twelve-Tone Equal Temperament** (12-TET).

Under 12-TET, an octave (which doubles the frequency) is divided into 12 semitones. The frequency of any note is calculated using the formula:

Frequency = Reference Frequency * 2^(n/12)

where n represents the number of semitones away from the reference note (A440).

Standard Guitar Tuning Frequencies

A standard 6-string guitar is tuned to E-A-D-G-B-E, spanning over two octaves. The table below outlines the exact notes, scientific pitch notation, and Hz frequency values for standard guitar tuning:

String Number Note Scientific Pitch Notation Frequency (Hz)
String 6 (Thickest) E E2 82.41 Hz
String 5 A A2 110.00 Hz
String 4 D D3 146.83 Hz
String 3 G G3 196.00 Hz
String 2 B B3 246.94 Hz
String 1 (Thinnest) E E4 329.63 Hz

Common Alternate Guitar Tunings

Musicians use alternate tunings to alter the instrument's resonance, make specific chords easier to play, or change the register of the strings:

1. Drop D Tuning

Drop D tuning is achieved by lowering the low E string (String 6) by a full step (two semitones) to D. The rest of the strings remain in standard tuning. This creates a low, heavy register and allows one-finger power chords across the three lowest strings.

  • Drop D Configuration: D2 (73.42 Hz) - A2 (110.00 Hz) - D3 (146.83 Hz) - G3 (196.00 Hz) - B3 (246.94 Hz) - E4 (329.63 Hz)

2. DADGAD Tuning

Highly popular in Celtic and folk music, DADGAD features a modal, open-sounding character. To achieve DADGAD, the 1st, 2nd, and 6th strings are detuned down by a full step.

  • DADGAD Configuration: D2 (73.42 Hz) - A2 (110.00 Hz) - D3 (146.83 Hz) - G3 (196.00 Hz) - A3 (220.00 Hz) - D4 (293.66 Hz)

3. Open G Tuning

Frequently used in blues and slide guitar, Open G sounds a G major chord when all strings are strummed open. The 1st, 5th, and 6th strings are detuned down a full step.

  • Open G Configuration: D2 (73.42 Hz) - G2 (98.00 Hz) - D3 (146.83 Hz) - G3 (196.00 Hz) - B3 (246.94 Hz) - D4 (293.66 Hz)

How Digital Pitch Tracking Works

The chromatic tuner in AudioMultiTool processes raw audio frames from your microphone using the Web Audio API. Standard Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) are often too coarse for precise bass tuning because they divide frequencies into rigid "bins" which lack cent-level resolution at lower ranges (like low E at 82.4 Hz).

To overcome this, our tuner uses a **Time-Domain Auto-Correlation Algorithm**:

  1. The incoming wave signal is copied and shifted by varying time delays (lags).
  2. The shifted signal is multiplied by the original signal to measure similarity.
  3. When the delay matches the exact period of the pitch, the auto-correlation score peaks.
  4. The fundamental frequency is calculated as the inverse of that time delay.

This allows cent-level precision (1 cent is 1/100th of a semitone), providing real-time visual feedback that shows you exactly how sharp or flat your string is relative to the target Hz pitch.
